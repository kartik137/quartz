
We wish to be able to leverage historical data to model the future. Linear Factor Models do so by using multiple RVs which each explain a portion of the target variable. They only use the MC of the RVs

Factor modeling does **not** try to capture causality (e.g. **why** will a stock return be positive). Instead it is purely a predictive tool. For example, we might find that GOOG goes up 2 bps whenever the fed funds rate decreases by 1 bp. This does not necessarily mean GOOG's price increases **because** of changes in EFFR. It only means we can predict GOOG via EFFR

- A **Linear Factor Model** decomposes a target variable $X$ completely by mean-covariance: $$\vec{X} = \vec{\alpha} + \beta \cdot \vec{Z} + \vec{\epsilon}$$
	where 
		- $X$ is the target variable vector (which we are trying to model)
		- $\alpha$ is the shift parameters (usually disregarded since they play no part in optimization)
		- $Z$ is the factor vector (independent variables correlated with $X$)
		- $\beta$ is the factor loading matrix (parameters that transfer the randomness of the factors $Z$ onto $X$)
		- $\epsilon$ are the residuals (randomness of $X$ that is not explained by the factors $Z$ alone)
- Again, LFMs only depend on mean-covariance which is what makes them powerful and why we care about them in the MC framework
	- Specifically, two LFMs that describe distributions with the same mean-covariance will be identical.

There are a few dimensions which can describe LFMs:
1. Type
	- Dominant-Residual: The goal is to maximize the fit of a linear combination of random variables to the target variables. That is, we want the factors $Z$ to explain most of the randomness in $X$, as measured by the $R^2$
	- Systematic-Idiosyncratic: We wish to uncover a simple structure in the distribution of the target variables. That is, we want the factors to be uncorrelated with the residuals (systematic) and the residuals to be uncorrelated with each other (idiosyncratic)
2. Factor specification
	- Whether the factors are specified exogenously, whether they are constructed endogenously from $X$, or they are hidden
3. Loading specification
	- Whether the loadings are constructed endogenously from $X$ or are hidden

# Principal Component Analysis
PCA is a type of unsupervised learning: we assume that a lower-dimensional set of hidden variables $H$ partially explain the observable targets $X$. The goal is to uncover these hidden relations using only the internal properties of the observables. 

The purpose of principal-components LFMs is dimensional reduction: given a larger number $n$ of target variables $X$,  we wish to explain these observable targets via a lower number $k << n$ of factors $Z$. This is best achieved when the target variables are highly correlated.

We can take the spectral decomposition of the covariance matrix (see the [appendix](Linear Factor Models#Appendix Spectral Decomposition)). The eigenspace represents the variance of the target variable $X$. The basis of the eigenspace are the eigenvectors with the associated eigenvalue representing the amount of variance of $X$ that the eigenvector captures. Therefore we can take the top $k$ eigenvectors (ranked by their associated eigenvalue) as a basis for a subspace. This subspace is the $k$-dimensional subspace which captures the most amount of variance of $X$. Note that high variance keeps the most amount of information of $X$.

Specifically, let the eigenvalues (ordered in decreasing value) be $\lambda_n$ and the corresponding eigenvectors be $e_n$. The subspace generated by $e_1$ captures the most variance of $X$ among all the 1-dimensional subspaces of $X$. Then $e_2$ captures the most variance of the n-1 dimensional space $X\setminus span(e_1)$ such that $e_2$ is uncorrelated with $e_!$. And so on

Another way to see this, is given any $k$ eigenvalues and eigenvectors, the corresponding $R^2$ is given by $$\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^k \lambda_i$$ where $N = \sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_n$  is a normalization factor. Thus to maximize $R^2$, we want the highest $k$ eigenvalues.

# Factor Analysis

Factor analysis LFMs are similar to principal component LFMs. However, the purpose of principal component LFMs is dimensionality reduction while the purpose of factor analysis LFMs is **complexity** reduction. 

Specifically, we split the residuals into two parts: $$X = \alpha + \beta\cdot Z + \epsilon^{idio} + \epsilon^{sys}$$
where $\epsilon^{idio}$ are the idiosyncratic residuals (which are uncorrelated with each other and need not have low variance), and $\epsilon^{sys}$ are the systematic residuals (which may be correlated to each other but have as low of variance as possible). Thus the systematic factors capture information about unimportant (and hidden) factors.

In finance, we generally assume the systematic residuals to be zero.


# Optimal Transport
Optimal Transport is a theory which can be use to estimate a distribution $F$ given an empirical distribution (although it has [many other applications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transportation_theory_(mathematics))). 

In general, transport theory analyzes continuous deformations between one mathematical object to another (akin to [homotopies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homotopy)). In our case, we are analyzing deformation between probability distributions. If you describe a metric (distance function between any two objects), one can determine an **optimal transport** which is the transport that minimizes the distance. 

As per Wikipedia:
	"Intuitively, if each distribution is viewed as a unit amount of earth (soil) piled on $M$ , the metric is the minimum "cost" of turning one pile into the other, which is assumed to be the amount of earth that needs to be moved times the mean distance it has to be moved"


In our case, we can use optimal transport to derive an estimates distribution $\bar{F}$ from an empirical distribution $\hat{F}$ by $$\bar{F} = \text{argmin}_F D\left(\hat{F}, F \right)$$  
The metric, $D$, that is typically used is the [Wasserstein distance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wasserstein_metric): $$D(F_1, F_2) = \int_\mathbb{R} \left| F_1(x) - F_2(x) \right| \,dx $$


The above definition is for the general case (the probabilistic framework). In the MC-framework, **Linear Optimal Transport**, we are specifically looking at affine transformations (i.e. MC-invariant transformations). In this case, the metric simplifies down to the mean squared error: $$\mathbb{E}\lVert X-Y \rVert_2^2$$

# Appendix: Spectral Decomposition

Unfortunately, many college linear algebra classes focus more on computation versus intuition. The following is an explanation of eigenvectors and eigenvalues which may not have been apparent in a computational-focused linear algebra course.

### Linear Transformations
Matrices represent linear transformations between two (finite-dimensional) vector spaces: $f: V \rightarrow W$. However, linear transformations can be arbitrarily complicated, e.g. $(x,y,z) \mapsto (3x-24y+7z, -54x+27y+34z, 26x-92y-10z)$. Sure this is easy in 3-dimensions, but it becomes much more difficult to evaluate (and moreover reason about) in higher dimensions.

### Direct Sums
Suppose $V_1, \dots , V_n$ are subspaces of $V$. Every element of $V$ can be written as $v_1 + \ldots v_n$ where $v_i \in V_i$. One special case is where each vector in $V$ can **uniquely** represented in the above form. We write $V_1 \bigoplus \ldots \bigoplus V_n$ if every vector can be written this way. Intuitively, this means each subspace is independent of all others: if $U$ and $W$ are subspaces of $V$ then $U + W$ is a direct sum if and only if $U \cap W = {0}$.

### Bases
We can represent a vector in a vector space using a basis $B$ of linearly-independent vectors. If $V$ is an n-dimensional vector space and $\mathcal{B} = (\vec{b}_1,\ldots, \vec{b}_n)$ is a set of linearly-independent vectors, we can represent any vector $\vec{v}$ as $\vec{v} = \sum_{i=1}^n a_i\cdot \vec{b}_i$. Equivalently, if $\mathcal{D} = (\vec{d}_1, \ldots, \vec{d}_n)$ is another set of linearly-independent vectors, we can represent $\vec{v}$ as $\vec{v} = \sum_{i=1}^n c_i\cdot \vec{d}_i$. A basis is simply a frame of reference for which we can quantitatively decompose a vector into a weighted sum of linearly-independent simple "basis" vectors.

Given two bases $\mathcal{B}$ and $\mathcal{C}$, there will be a linear transformation $P$ which turns a vector space represented under $\mathcal{B}$ to the vector space represented under $\mathcal{C}$. This transformation is called the "change-of-basis" transformation. One can show that a transformation $P$ is a change-of-basis transformation if and only if $P$ is invertible (i.e. there is a transformation $Q$ such that $PQ=I$).

### Eigenspaces
It would be great if a complicated linear transformation could be simplified. This is the case for diagonalizable transformation (which have a matrix which is diagonal under some basis). In this case, there is a change of basis for $V$ such that the linear transform can be represented as $\sum_{i=1}^n v_i\vec{e}_i \mapsto \sum_{i=1}^n (\lambda_iv_i)\vec{e}_i$. We call $\lambda_i$ eigenvalues and $e_i$ eigenvectors. In other words, the transformation acts on each dimension (by expanding/contracting it) independently.


Take a diagonal matrix 
$$D = \begin{bmatrix}
\lambda_1 & 0 & 0\\
0 & \ddots & 0 \\
0 & 0 & \lambda_n
\end{bmatrix}
$$
Notice that on any vector $v = (v_1, v_2, v_3)$, $D$ acts on $v$ by simply scaling each component:
$(v_1, v_2, v_3) \mapsto (\lambda_1v_1, \lambda_2v_2, \lambda_3v_3)$. This is effectively the simplest non-trivial linear transformation of a space you can have.

Now note that a linear transformation may not be able to be represented as a diagonal matrix under one basis but may be diagonal under another basis. In particular, a linear transformation $T$ is called **diagonalizable** if there is some invertible matrix $P$ (i.e. a change-of-basis transformation from bases $\mathcal{B}$ to $\mathcal{C}$) such that $P^{-1}TP$ is diagonal. When $P^{-1}TP$ acts on a vector $\vec{v}$, it first converts the vector from basis $\mathcal{B}$ to basis $\mathcal{C}$ using $P$, applies the transformation $T$ under the basis $\mathcal{C}$, and then converts the resulting vector back from basis $\mathcal{C}$ to basis $\mathcal{B}$ using $P^{-1}$. 


Thus, if a linear transformation is diagonalizable, there exists some basis under which the transformation acts on each dimension independently by contracting or expanding that dimension. In other words, there exists a change-of-basis transformation such that we can represent $V$ as a direct sum of 1-dimensional subspaces, $V=V_1\oplus \ldots \oplus V_n$, such that the transformation acts on each subspace independently.
